# Round 13 idea notes for the English writer

These ideas come from the German pool: situations, objects and joke shapes the Grantler
has made work that the English pool hasn't touched. They're notes, not lines. The German
wording is left out on purpose. Write your own joke, in the screen's voice: the TRMNL,
a little too pleased with itself. A Grantler grumbling about strangers usually becomes
the screen looking down on the situation from indoors, or (at 11) turning it on the reader.

Before you start:
- **What's thin in English right now:** `flavour_11.hot` is empty. `now_10.cold` has 6
  lines (German has 15) and `now_10.cool` has 2. `now_11.night` has 5 (German 11).
  `flavour_vf_10/11.fickle` and `flavour_vf_11.cold` are empty too, but the owner has
  vetoed almost every "three drawings" joke so far, so those aren't recommended here.
- **What the English pool hardly does:** comment on *other people*. Almost every English
  line is about the screen or "you". German stars come from public scenes the reader has
  seen. Several notes below are about the street, the train or the neighbours.
- **Everyone who reads English gets the same line** (only topical lines have regions). An
  image that only a Londoner knows will baffle someone in Ohio. Pick details that cross
  the Atlantic, or ones that read clearly even when unfamiliar.
- **Don't assume a wall** (this one matters for the drill note), no clothing advice, and
  evening lines must not guess the reader's plans.

## E1. A dusting of snow brings the whole country to a halt
- **Idea:** a token amount of snow falls and the country reacts as if it were a
  blizzard: alarmed headlines, cancelled trains, closed schools. The joke is how small the snow is next to how big the reaction is. A
  second angle: everyone panic-buys before it arrives (in the US, the bread-and-milk run).
- **Where:** `now_10.snow` (drawing: snow outfit, snow falling). The panic-buying version
  also fits `flavour_10.snow` under "It starts snowing this afternoon".
- **Why:** three German variants of this are starred, the most stars of any German idea,
  and a separate line about the railways folding is starred too. English snow lines are all about how
  snow looks on e-ink. Nothing about how people react to it.
- **Bend for the screen:** the screen is the one thing that doesn't shut down. It runs no
  timetable and never closes, and it can be smug about it. Or it notices that it's the
  most reliable service in town today.
- **Trap:** the UK version (rail excuses, gritters, school closures) is the strongest, so
  keep the details general enough for US readers. Steer clear of the vetoed "you'll act
  like it's the first snow ever".

## E2. Hot-day follow-up: the reader's version of "water"
- **Idea:** the forecast said to bring water. The reader will bring something they
  insist counts: iced coffee, a pint, a large soda.
- **Where:** `flavour_11.hot` (empty in English) and `flavour_10.hot`. These sit under
  "It's hot all day. Bring water and stay in the shade." / "Bring water" / c_relief.
- **Why:** German has this at both levels and it builds directly on the fact line, which
  is the owner's rule for follow-ups (round 11). It also fills an empty slot.
- **Bend:** the screen insists it was very precise and it said *water*. Or at 11 it
  calls out the reader's loophole as if it has seen the receipts.
- **Trap:** it has to name what the fact line asked for, so it only works in the groups
  where the fact line mentions water.

## E3. The late-night fridge raid
- **Idea:** after 22:00 the reader drifts to the fridge, and the screen knows.
- **Where:** `now_11.night` (drawing: night, current outfit; bed talk allowed).
- **Why:** two German lines about it are starred. English night lines are about blue
  light, power and going to bed. Snacks don't come up at all.
- **Bend:** the screen as the all-seeing household witness that does nothing but judge.
  It doesn't eat, so it's above all this.
- **Trap:** don't place the screen ("I can see the kitchen from here"). The owner cut
  wall and location assumptions. "I see everything" works anywhere.

## E4. "One more episode" and the other screen in the room
- **Idea:** the reader promises themselves one more episode, several episodes ago.
- **Where:** `now_11.night`.
- **Why:** starred twice in German. In English it's a natural fit for a screen voice,
  because the screen can feel rivalry with the TV or the streaming service. The service
  asks whether you're still watching. This screen doesn't need to ask.
- **Bend:** jealousy plus smugness: the TV gets the hours, this screen gets the glance
  before bed. Or the screen counting the episodes.
- **Trap:** English already has "the one screen that won't keep you up" (no blue light).
  Don't redo that. Make it about the reader lying to themselves, or about the TV.

## E5. The person in shorts in the middle of winter
- **Idea:** whatever the cold, somebody out there is in shorts. In the UK it's the
  postman (an icon), the lad at the bus stop or the dad at the football.
- **Where:** `now_10.cold` (drawing: winter coat or more).
- **Why:** starred in German, and a reworded twin was kept. English cold lines never look
  at anyone else.
- **Bend:** the screen observes from indoors with a mix of horror and professional
  interest: it drew the coat, and someone out there ignored the drawing.
- **Trap:** hedge it ("probably", "somewhere"). The owner added those words himself
  ("Hedge a guess", round 8). Don't make it the reader at level 10.

## E6. The backhanded compliment for being bundled up
- **Idea:** the reader looks their best when most of them is covered: hood up, scarf
  over the face.
- **Where:** `now_11.cold` (drawing: winter coat, often hat/scarf/gloves).
- **Why:** this was starred three times in German, on three separate votes. English
  11-cold lines are about noses, noises and ears, and none of them is about looks.
- **Bend:** the screen fancies itself a style critic because it's so pleased with its own
  look. It approves of the outfit because of what the outfit hides.
- **Trap:** the joke is about the garments drawn on screen, so it isn't clothing advice.
  Don't name a garment the drawing may not show. "Wrapped up" is safe.

## E7. You don't deserve this weather
- **Idea:** a perfect day has been handed to someone who'll waste it, and there were
  more deserving people.
- **Where:** `now_11.nice` (drawing: light outfit, sun).
- **Why:** two German versions are starred. English 11-nice is all about pretending to go
  for a run and staying in. "Deserving" is a fresh angle.
- **Bend:** the screen acts as if it hands out the weather, or as if it had someone better
  in mind for today. It fits the too-pleased-with-itself voice: it delivered a masterpiece
  to the wrong address.
- **Trap:** keep it on the reader. "Others deserve it" alone reads as a general remark.

## E8. The first barbecue, the full pub garden, everyone acting like they're on holiday
- **Idea:** on the first proper nice day, neighbours drag out the barbecue, every pub
  garden is full by noon, and people sit outside cafés as if they were in Spain.
- **Where:** `now_10.nice`. The "first one of the year" version wants `{M:04,05}`.
  The everyone-on-holiday version works all summer.
- **Why:** German stars the neighbour already out with the barbecue tongs, and keeps the
  ice-cream-parlour and beer-garden versions. Concrete scenes are what earn German stars
  ("Concrete scenes earn stars", round 4). English nice lines are all the screen
  talking about itself.
- **Bend:** the screen can't go and isn't invited. Or it's the only thing in the house
  that won't get sunburnt, smell of charcoal, or pretend it lives on the Med.
- **Trap:** beer gardens are German. Pub garden (UK) or backyard cookout (US) translate
  the scene. Don't reference German beer culture.

## E9. The weather finally matches your face
- **Idea:** a grey, wet day is the first weather that matches the reader's usual mood or
  expression. A second angle: the rain can't make the hair any worse.
- **Where:** `now_11.wet` (drawing: rain outfit with umbrella).
- **Why:** starred twice in German (face), and the hair one is starred too. English
  11-wet is about umbrellas and checking the phone, never about the reader's looks or
  mood.
- **Bend:** the screen is proud of its own grey ("Grey was my look" is starred in cold).
  The grey sky and grey screen look classy. The grey reader doesn't.
- **Trap:** don't repeat the screen's grey-look line. Use it as background and keep the
  joke on the reader.

## E10. Halloween: pretending not to be home
- **Idea:** when the doorbell rings on Halloween, the reader turns the lights off and
  hides from trick-or-treaters.
- **Where:** `flavour_11.theme_spooky` or `flavour_10.theme_spooky`. Theme lines show
  under the forecast on a steady day only, so no weather words.
- **Why:** it's in the German theme pool, and the owner liked the doorbell direction in a
  vetoed German line ("direction is good, doesn't land"). In English, hiding from
  trick-or-treaters is a far more familiar ritual than in Germany.
- **Bend:** e-ink doesn't glow, so the screen is the perfect accomplice because it won't
  give the house away. Or it judges the reader crouched below the window.
- **Trap:** English already has the "ate the candy by eight" joke. Don't stack a second
  candy joke.

## E11. The commute in the heat smells of everyone
- **Idea:** on a hot day, the train or bus smells of every single passenger.
- **Where:** `now_10.hot` (drawing: shorts/T-shirt, sun).
- **Why:** starred in German. English hot lines are all about the screen keeping cool.
- **Bend:** the screen has no nose and has never been so grateful for it. Or its commute
  is zero (already used for evening rush hour, so don't repeat that one).
- **Trap:** the Tube (no air conditioning on old lines) and the NYC subway platform are
  both vivid. Say "the train" or "the bus" so both work.

## E12. The heating standoff (seasonal)
- **Idea:** the yearly argument over when the heating goes on: the house rule "not
  before November", the person in a blanket like a cape, the thermostat war at the
  office.
- **Where:** `now_10.cool` (only 2 lines!) or `now_10.cold`, with `{M:10,11}`.
- **Why:** German keeps a line about fighting over the office heating, and stars the
  people who say cold toughens you up while their own heating is on full. English has
  nothing on heating at all.
- **Bend:** the screen is neutral in the war, needs no heat and keeps score. Or it notes
  that it's the only thing in the house not asking for the heating.
- **Trap:** English already has "I run cool on principle". Don't make it another "I'm
  cool" line. The joke is the household politics.

## E13. Browsing flights to somewhere warm (seasonal)
- **Idea:** in the depths of winter the reader looks at flights south again and books
  nothing.
- **Where:** `now_11.cold` with `{M:01,02}`.
- **Why:** starred in German (dream of the south, then just go). In English it's a
  well-known ritual: Brits and Spain or the Canaries, Americans and Florida.
- **Bend:** the screen has never been anywhere and never will be, and it's still less
  stuck than the reader. Or it has seen this search before, every January.
- **Trap:** no destinations that date. Keep it generic ("somewhere warm").

## E14. Getting dressed takes so long the season changes
- **Idea:** comic exaggeration of how long it takes to get all the winter layers on,
  measured against something absurdly long.
- **Where:** `now_11.cold` (drawing: the full winter kit).
- **Why:** starred in German, with a kept twin. English cold lines don't do exaggeration
  over time.
- **Bend:** measure it in the screen's units: how many times it has refreshed while you
  looked for a glove. That gives the self-absorbed screen the punch line.
- **Trap:** keep it to the garments in the drawing.

## E15. Even the snowman has more personality
- **Idea:** compare the reader to a snowman, and the snowman wins.
- **Where:** `now_11.snow` (drawing: snow outfit).
- **Why:** starred in German, with a kept twin. English 11-snow lines are about falling and
  dripping, never a put-down.
- **Bend:** the screen rates the snowman's presence ("great stillness, very matte")
  above the reader's. The screen admires anything that stands still and looks good, so
  the snowman is its kind of guy.
- **Trap:** keep it short. A long setup kills it.

## E16. Structure: quote the stock saying, then puncture it
- **Shape:** quote the thing people always say about the weather, then knock it down,
  usually by pointing out who says it. In German this shape is starred three ways: the
  saying about no bad weather, only wrong clothes; cold toughening you up, said by those
  with the heating on; "you have to go out in weather like this".
- **Where:** `now_10.wet`, `now_10.cold`, `now_10.nice`.
- **English material:** "lovely weather for ducks", "bracing", "at least it's a dry
  cold", "it's not the heat, it's the humidity", the wrong-clothes saying itself.
- **Bend:** the screen *is* the clothes-picking authority. If there's no bad weather,
  only bad clothes, then the screen solved weather this morning and would like the credit.
- **Trap:** English already does this with the reader's own words ('crisp', 'proper
  snow'). Use folk sayings, not the reader's words, so it doesn't repeat.

## E17. Structure: the public-ritual prophecy
- **Shape:** predict what *everyone* out there is about to do or say, as if the screen has
  watched it happen every year. English material: the first frost photo of a car
  windscreen, the queue at the ice-cream van, someone at the bus stop saying it's
  turned out nice again. German uses this shape constantly ("any minute now, someone
  will…") and it carries several stars. English almost always predicts "you".
- **Where:** any `now_10` mood, especially `cool` and `cold` where English is thin.
- **Bend:** the screen watches the city like a bored know-it-all who has seen this
  episode before.
- **Trap:** it needs a punch line after the prediction. "An idea isn't a joke" (round 6).

## E18. On again, off again
- **Idea:** the layer goes off, then back on, like the reader's relationships.
- **Where:** `flavour_11.offon` under "The jacket can come off this afternoon, but you'll
  want it back tonight."
- **Why:** the German offon-11 version is in the pool in two wordings. English "on-again,
  off-again" is its own native idiom, so the writer has an English route in.
- **Bend:** the screen as the gossip who knows the reader's love life. Or it says the
  jacket is the most committed relationship the reader has.
- **Trap:** don't just state the idiom. The turn has to land on the reader.

## E19. Neighbour noise: the evening drill, the late laundry
- **Idea:** some neighbour always starts drilling in the evening. After 22:00, somebody's
  washing machine or dryer thumps through the building.
- **Where:** drill: `now_10.evening`. Laundry: `now_10.night`.
- **Why:** both kept in German, and the round-6 evening notes say observations about
  other people work in evenings. English evening and night lines are only about screens.
- **Bend:** the screen is the quietest appliance in the building: it makes no noise,
  uses no power and holds its picture in silence.
- **Trap:** a drill invites "mounted on the wall" jokes, and the owner vetoed wall
  assumptions. Leave the screen's own mounting out.

## E20. Chore weather (with a seasonal twist)
- **Idea:** a dull, dry day is perfect for chores the reader won't do: the windows, the
  laundry on the line ("a good drying day" is a very British phrase), and in autumn the
  leaves to rake.
- **Where:** `now_11.cool` (drawing: jacket, dry). Add a `{M:10,11}` leaf-raking version.
- **Why:** two versions starred in German (great window-cleaning weather, and yours
  need it). English cool lines are about being average and blending in.
- **Bend:** the screen is the one clean pane of glass in the house, or it needs no
  maintenance and is smug about it.
- **Trap:** only dry days. Don't put it where the drawing may show rain.

Month tags fire everywhere, including the ~2.7% of devices in the southern hemisphere. Social-calendar moments (holidays, shopping, school) travel better than weather-season ones.

## Don't carry these over
- **Glühwein, Krampus, Nikolaus, Reformation Day, Stoßlüften** (opening every window wide to air the flat): German culture. A
  UK mulled-wine line is possible but niche. Skip.
- **The lost-property office, the rain radar, the umbrella left on the train, the people
  who say "it got chilly", surprise at snow:** English already covers these
  well enough (losing the umbrella, everyone checking the screen, acting shocked).
- **"Three drawings" jokes** for fickle: the owner vetoed about ten English attempts.
